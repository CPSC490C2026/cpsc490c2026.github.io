CPSC 490C: Critical Computing

Course Description

A critical study of computing as embedded in a larger system with a colonial history and present. This course identifies power structures within computing through its histories, its “postcolonial” and decolonial critiques, and the abstraction boundaries that keep its costs out of view.

Prerequisite: 3rd or 4th year standing

Learning Objectives

  1. Identify power structures within computing
  2. Situate contemporary computing practices within histories of colonialism
  3. Evaluate who the “defaults” of a computer system serve and who is marginalized by them
  4. Engage in productive disagreement around complex ethical questions in computing
  5. Trace a computing system’s abstraction boundaries to its material, labor, and environmental realities
  6. Construct an original critical analysis of a computing system

Course Structure

We will have one 3-hour lecture slot each week. Most weeks, we plan on using up to 2 hours of this slot (more information on this below). A typical week has the following structure:

  1. Readings assigned

    At the end of each lecture, readings for the following week’s topic are assigned. (No readings are assigned before the first week.)

  2. Reading and note-taking

    Before lecture, students complete the readings, take notes, and post questions or comments to our discussion forum.

  3. Lecture

    Lecture opens with a short framing of the week’s topic, then moves into group discussion grounded in the readings and the questions students raised beforehand. Most weeks, this core portion wraps up within the first 2 hours, leaving the remaining time for unstructured conversation and reading.

  4. Discussion summary

    Each group submits a short summary of their discussion by the end of the day.

In addition, we will have assignments throughout the term. See the Assessment section below for details on those.

Assessment

Participation 10%

We will have weekly group discussions during lecture time. These will allow you to get to know your peers and engage in productive conversations around complex topics with them. Each group takes notes on their conversation and submits them by the end of the day.

Graded for completion.

Peer Evaluation 10%

There will be opportunities to evaluate your peers’ work a few times throughout this course. These will be evaluated on thoughtfulness of feedback and engagement with your peer’s work.

Readings 15%

Once a week, you will be assigned readings to complete and take notes on. You will share these notes and any questions you had while reading on our discussion forum. These comments will serve as the basis for our in-class discussion that week.

Graded for meaningful completion.

Reflections 15%

2-3 times during the course, you will reflect on the course so far. What have you learned? How is the pacing of the course? Are there topics not on the schedule that you would like to see covered?

These will be opportunities to give feedback to the course staff and help shape the rest of the course going forward.

Graded for meaningful completion.

Tracing through a computer system 15%

You will choose a computer system of interest to you and trace through its layers of abstraction to identify its impact on resources, labor, and the environment.

Peer evaluated according to a rubric and given feedback.

Note: This is expected to be what you build on for your final project.

Final Project - Critical Analysis of a Computer System 20%

You will construct an original critical analysis of a computer system. We encourage you to build upon the work you did for the previous “Tracing through a computer system” assignment. Rather than just tracing through the layers of abstraction, this project should use a more focused analysis of the system and the context in which it exists to make an argument.

The project should:

  • Identify key structural concerns raised by the system.
  • Engage deeply with 1-3 of these concerns.
  • Take a clear position on them, informed by the histories, critiques, and frameworks
    we discuss in the course.
  • Support that position and address at least one counterargument.

Some example formats are listed below but you’re encouraged to talk with course staff if you would like to have your project in a different format.

  • Paper (2000 - 3000 words)
  • Zine (8-16 pages)
  • Podcast (15-25 minutes)
  • Video (8-12 minutes)

Projects will be graded by external graders where possible. If external graders aren’t available, we’ll fall back to peer grading according to the same rubric.

Presentation 15%

Presentations are done in groups of up to 4. You may choose your own groups; anyone not in a group by the deadline will be assigned one by course staff.

Each group’s presentation should:

  • Briefly walk through each group member’s individual final project.
  • Articulate a broader framework for thinking critically about computing that ties the group’s individual projects together. This can either be a shared view if the group reaches a consensus, or an expression of the different points of view within the group.

These will be presented in-class and graded by peers.

Bonus Points

Bonus points are intended to recognize contributions to the success of the course community as a whole and learning that goes above and beyond. These are not bonus points that contribute directly toward your grade, however. Direct rewards from bonus points will be small and sometimes fun and non-grade-related. You should go out and earn bonus points, but mostly because you already want to go above and beyond and help others’ learning.

An example of a Bonus Point Accumulating Activity (BPAA) is spending time engaging with something relevant to the course topics that you’re passionate about (inspired by WWF’s Give an Hour for Earth campaign). We’ll do show-and-tell at the start of lectures if you’d like to share what you’ve been doing!
